Colors are everywhere. They dominate our perception of reality, our emotions, and our understanding of beauty and art. The way colors work is not straightforward, but surprisingly simple. Let’s explore the world of color theory.

The Three Essentials

There are three essentials to understand when it comes to learning color theory. Every color can be described with those three qualities. Making sense of them helps us later when looking at some more advanced aspects of colors.

Hue

The first essential quality to know is hue. Hue is essentially just the answer to the question “Which color is it?”.

Is it red, yellow, green, purple, blue, or white?

Simple, right?

Colors in different hues.

Lightness (or Value)

The second quality is equally straightforward. It’s lightness, or value. It answers the question “How bright is it?”.

Darker variations of a color are called “shade”, lighter ones are called “tint”.

Colors with different levels of lightness.

Saturation

Finally, there is saturation. Saturation is about the question “How intense is the color?”.

Is it vibrant? Is it greyish?

Colors with different levels of saturation.

Color Models

Now, we understand the three basic qualities of color. Each color can be described in hue, lightness/value, and saturation. This model of describing color even has a name: HSL (standing for hue, saturation, and lightness) or HSV (if value instead of lightness is used).

For example, this color has an HSL value of 226°, 30%, and 51%.

The 226° stands for the hue, which is described by a 360° color wheel starting and ending again at red.

The Color Wheel.

The 30% stands for the level of saturation. In this case, the color is more on the grayish rather than the vibrant side.

The 51% stands for the level of lightness, which is neither especially light nor especially dark for this color.

Apart from the HSL and HSV models, there are also other color models. The most famous ones are RGB and HEX, which both describe how much red, green, and blue is in each color. Those systems are mostly used for digital design, as screens consist of tiny red, green, and blue lights.

For example, the color above would be (94, 111, 168) in RGB. Where the first value stands for the amount of red, the second for green, and the third for blue. The maximum value is 255 for each color.

The Meaning of Color

Different colors evoke different emotions. There’s a lot to read and find out about the meanings of color. The discussion becomes abstract and esoteric very quickly, but we can come to some basic psychological conclusions about the meaning of color.

Green, for example, conveys closeness to nature, integrity, and health.

Blue, on the other hand, gives a feeling of trust, calmness, wisdom, and competence.

It shouldn’t come as a surprise, then, that hospitals, the place where competence, trust, and healing are essential, are often designed in tones of blue and green.

Color Combinations

Since we now understand the properties of a color, we can proceed to harmoniously combine them.

The simplest combination we can do is monochromatic.

Triadic

The last popular combination is the triadic one. Here, one chooses three colors that look like a triangle on the color wheel (see below).

All in all, there is no right or wrong when it comes to choosing colors. Hue is also not the only factor relevant to combinations; saturation and lightness are equally important. Those models are valuable guides, but usually, a degree of experimentation is necessary. This is also what makes it fun.

Color Context

Finally, let’s look at color context. We are going to illustrate this concept with a photo I made yesterday.

What color are the brightest leaves on this tree (in the white circle)? A strong, bright green?

No. A pale yellow. Those leaves only seem green due to the context.

Generally, the color hues in warm, bright images like the one above tend to be lighter and yellowish. In other images, like this painting of mine, the grey and white mountains appear in shades of blue.

The Power of Color

Color is subject to perception. Science sees it as probable that we all experience colors differently. So, my blue might be totally different from yours (mine is better than yours 😁).

Since color and perception are so closely related, the combinations, contexts, and use cases of color often have something to do with emotion. Color can determine whether something seems harmonious, trustworthy, or aesthetic.

This is the power color has, whether that is in web design, a Renaissance painting, or a hospital.
